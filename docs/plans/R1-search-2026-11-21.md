# Search summaries of 2026-11-21, on rocket launches in seismic data

Three web searches the lead ran on 2026-11-21 before drafting the plan. What follows is what the search tool's summaries said. **The lead opened none of the pages.** Every statement here is a lead for the LCH parcel to check against the page itself.

## Search 1: "rocket launch recorded on seismometers seismo-acoustic signal Falcon 9 sonic boom seismic network study"

- **arXiv 2502.01982, "Rocketquake Seismology with a Falcon 9 Rocket Source"** (https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.01982). The summary: Schuster and colleagues recorded seismic vibrations from a Falcon 9 launch at Vandenberg with nine three-component seismometers placed about 7 km from the pad, with one more at roughly 15 km. The far sensor did not capture any discernible body wave arrivals. Prior work cited in the paper notes that launch or landing of a Falcon 9 produces strong acoustic airwaves detectable beyond 10 km.
- **McNutt et al., AGU 2017, "Infrasound and Seismic Recordings of Rocket Launches from Kennedy Space Center, 2016-2017"** (https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017AGUFM.S44A..01M). The summary: a temporary seismic-infrasound network recorded several launches; the abstract reports a clear Doppler shift in seismic and infrasound spectrograms from all launches, with lower frequencies occurring later as the rocket moves away.
- **Thompson et al. 2017, "Infrasound and Seismic Analysis of the SpaceX Falcon9 Explosion Sequence of 1-September-2016"** (https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/geo_facpub/2182). The summary: the seismic record from the 2016 pad explosion consists mainly of air-coupled Rayleigh waves with frequencies of 5-23 Hz.
- **A Caltech Southern California Seismic Network page** (https://tungsten2.gps.caltech.edu/?p=4154). The summary: operators reported that a 2021 Falcon 9 launch from Vandenberg was detected, and that the signal lacks distinct seismic phases, which distinguishes it from an earthquake.
- **An EGU 2026 abstract** (https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU26/EGU26-6974.html). The summary: infrasound and seismoacoustic recordings were used to reconstruct the trajectory of a February 2025 Falcon 9 upper-stage reentry.
- The summary's own note: none of these is a study of sonic booms recorded by seismometers.

## Search 2: "Vandenberg Falcon 9 launch sonic boom seismic stations Southern California Seismic Network detection distance"

- The summary's own note: nothing was found on Southern California Seismic Network stations or on how far they detect Falcon 9 booms. The results were acoustic studies and news.
- **Launch cadence**, from a news page: in 2024 the Falcon 9 was launched 46 times from Vandenberg.
- **A Vandenberg-funded acoustic study** (https://www.edhat.com/news/vandenberg-sfb-leads-study-on-ascent-sonic-booms-in-space-launches/, and https://acoustics.org/tag/rocket/): about 25 monitoring stations across 500 square miles of California's central coast; a later report says 23 launches tracked and 477 acoustic recordings collected since mid-2024. One page's title says the season affects whether a sonic boom is heard during ascent.
- News pages say booms have been reported along more than 100 miles of coastline on some launches and only near the base on others.

## Search 3: "public rocket launch log liftoff times to the second dataset GCAT McDowell launch list download"

- **GCAT, Jonathan McDowell's General Catalog of Artificial Space Objects** (https://planet4589.org/space/gcat/web/launch/). The summary: launch lists are published for download as text and TSV, split by type (orbital launches, failures, suborbital, pad explosions). There is a separate Launch Time References file, and each launch carries a citation for the source of its launch time. Released under CC-BY; the suggested short citation is "data from GCAT (J. McDowell, planet4589.org/space/gcat)".
- The summary's own note: it did not confirm whether times are recorded to the second.
